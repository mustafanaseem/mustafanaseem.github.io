<h2 class="case-title">Har Zindagi: Making childhood immunization records useful to families and vaccinators</h2>
<div class="case-subtitle">Punjab, Pakistan · 2015&ndash;2017 (DFID District Delivery Challenge Fund)</div>

<div class="case-intro">
A child's immunization record has to work for several people at once: a caregiver keeping track of the next visit, a vaccinator recording what happened, and a health system trying to identify children who have been missed. As principal investigator of Har Zindagi &mdash; "Every Life Matters" in Urdu &mdash; I led a team working with Punjab's Expanded Program on Immunization to redesign that experience, connecting a more legible physical card with an Android app that vaccinators used to create digital records in the field.
</div>

<figure class="case-figure">
  <img src="{{ base_path }}/images/harzindagi.png" alt="Photograph of the redesigned Har Zindagi immunization booklet, open to the 6-week visit. The left page shows date-of-visit fields for OPV-1, Penta-1, PCV 10-1, and Rotavirus-1, each color coded and paired with an icon of the body system the vaccine protects. The right page marks the child's developmental stage in Urdu ('6-week-old child') and illustrates three milestones with pictorial captions." />
  <figcaption>The third and deployed prototype of the redesigned immunization card, open to the 6-week visit. Each vaccine row uses a distinct color and an icon of the body system it protects; the facing page marks the child's developmental stage. Photograph from the project team.</figcaption>
</figure>

<h2 class="case-h2">1. The problem</h2>

<div class="case-body">
<p>Punjab's existing paper immunization card was a single, small-format page. Field interviews with 69 caregivers and 5 vaccinators found that roughly a third of caregivers could not read the next-visit date as vaccinators had written it, and about the same share could not follow the schedule printed on the back. Caregivers were not one group: most read Urdu or Punjabi and most kept the card carefully, but instructions in small print were hard for anyone to use, and pictorial guidance for lower-literate readers was largely absent (Batool et al., ICTD '16).</p>

<p>On the vaccinator side, Punjab had already deployed <em>eVaccs</em> in 2014, an Android app that tracked vaccinator attendance and GPS location. eVaccs was designed for monitoring, not for creating a child record: vaccinators still hand-wrote each visit on the paper card and in two paper registers, and the app forced records to be uploaded one at a time before a vaccinator could check out for the day &mdash; a hard requirement in rural areas with unreliable connectivity, where a single record could take up to ten minutes to upload (Razaq et al., ACM DEV '16).</p>

<p>Digitizing the vaccinator's work alone had not made the record understandable or durable for families. We needed to design for the people on both sides of a vaccination visit, and for the handoff between them.</p>
</div>

<h2 class="case-h2">2. Design innovation: the card</h2>

<div class="case-body">
<p>The card went through three prototypes over roughly two months of interviews and field testing across Lahore, Murree, Sargodha, Nankana Sahib, Sheikhupura, and Sahiwal. Each prototype was tested with caregivers at vaccination centers and with state-appointed vaccinators.</p>

<h3 class="case-h3">Prototype 1: separate fields per visit, back-of-card advice</h3>
<p>The first iteration gave each visit its own row and each vaccine its own field, added fields for off-schedule polio and measles campaigns, and moved parent instructions to the back of the card in larger local-language text. Field feedback surfaced two problems: including weight-and-height fields expanded the vaccinator's workload into a task that Punjab assigns to Lady Health Workers, and caregivers still confused dates of administered vaccines with next-visit dates.</p>

<h3 class="case-h3">Prototype 2: waterproof booklet with carbonless copies and a next-visit slit</h3>
<p>The second iteration turned the card into a waterproof, zip-locked booklet with six carbonless duplicate pages &mdash; one per visit &mdash; that would be torn off and OCR/OMR scanned at the Union Council. It introduced a Health Management Information System (HMIS) identifier for the first time and cut a slit in the outer cover so caregivers could see the next-visit date without opening the booklet. Field testing showed the design would collapse under real workflow: Union Council offices could not absorb the scanning load, delicate carbonless paper would not survive rural handling, and caregivers found the layout more overwhelming, not less.</p>

<h3 class="case-h3">Prototype 3: the deployed booklet</h3>
<p>The third prototype kept the passport-form factor and put the design work into features that had a clear reason:</p>

<ul class="design-list">
  <li><strong>One page per visit, color coded.</strong> Six distinct pages, one per scheduled visit, each in a different color that also appears on the corresponding tab in the vaccinator app. The color is what tells a vaccinator, at a glance, that the paper page and the app screen are the same visit.</li>
  <li><strong>Developmental-stage cue on each page.</strong> Each visit page marks the child's age &mdash; "6-week-old child," "10-week-old child," and so on &mdash; and illustrates a small set of expected milestones for that age, so the card reads as a growth booklet rather than a receipt.</li>
  <li><strong>Icons of body systems protected by each vaccine.</strong> Every vaccine row is paired with an icon of the organ or system it protects, so the reader can associate the vaccine with what it does without needing to read the vaccine name.</li>
  <li><strong>Booklet materials that signal a document worth keeping.</strong> The team chose more durable paper, a passport-shaped fold, and an embossed Government of Punjab monogram on the cover. Caregiver interviews had already established that card retention was high (94 percent kept it carefully) because the card was needed for services like school admission; the design leaned into that.</li>
  <li><strong>Immunization page for the mother.</strong> A dedicated page in the same booklet holds the mother's own vaccinations, so a family does not have to manage a separate card.</li>
  <li><strong>An NFC chip embedded in the card.</strong> The chip stored the child's unique identifier and next-due date, and could be read and re-written by the vaccinator's Android app. This is the feature that connected the paper card to the digital system.</li>
</ul>

<p>Field testing of prototype 3 with 30 vaccinators and 10 caregivers found that most vaccinators &mdash; 80 percent &mdash; liked the physical material and official look, and 63 percent found the new card better for surfacing relevant information. On the caregiver side the results were more mixed: 60 percent preferred the physical form to the older card, but only 30 percent of semi-literate mothers could read the instructions and only 20 percent could understand the pictorial developmental guidance. The pictorial system was a research finding as much as a design win: pictograms alone were not sufficient without accompanying oral or in-person explanation.</p>

<div class="limits-note">
<strong>What the team shipped versus what a government adopts.</strong> The NFC-linked booklet was the research prototype. According to Naseem's 2021 research statement, the Government of Punjab later adopted a modified version of the card design; investigating which specific features were retained &mdash; and which were dropped &mdash; is one of the open questions on this page.
</div>
</div>

<h2 class="case-h2">3. Design innovation: the vaccinator app</h2>

<div class="case-body">
<p>The Android app went through three iterations before it was ready for field testing. A few decisions carry most of the weight, and each traces back to something the team learned in the field rather than to a feature list on paper.</p>

<h3 class="case-h3">Records could be created offline and uploaded when a signal returned</h3>
<p>The previous eVaccs app required each record to upload before the vaccinator could continue, and refused to let vaccinators check out until every record had uploaded. In rural Punjab this meant vaccinator attendance depended on cellular coverage. Har Zindagi added delay-tolerant local storage and a single-click batch upload that resumed automatically when the network came back &mdash; and, at the vaccinators' request, kept working when the app was operating fully offline (Razaq et al., ACM DEV '16).</p>

<h3 class="case-h3">Tapping the card opened the right visit</h3>
<p>Scanning the NFC chip on a returning child's card jumped the app directly to the correct visit tab rather than making the vaccinator scroll through six tabs to find the current one. When a family arrived without their card, vaccinators could search the central database in two ways: an online lookup when connectivity allowed, and an SMS-based lookup when it did not. This directly addressed the lost-card scenario that vaccinators identified in interviews.</p>

<h3 class="case-h3">The card and the app spoke the same visual language</h3>
<p>The color of each visit tab in the app matched the color of the matching page in the card. The idea was to make the paper record and the digital record look like the same document from two directions, so a vaccinator filling both was not switching mental models.</p>

<h3 class="case-h3">"Plan My Day" was removed after the vaccinators said it did not fit their work</h3>
<p>Version 1 of the app included a route-optimization feature that used shortest-path algorithms to plan a vaccinator's day. Vaccinators explained that they already had pre-assigned field plans agreed with their supervisors, so an algorithmic route was not useful. The team removed the feature. That decision, more than any of the ones that shipped, is what the paper argues is the real design lesson: features that look good to authorities and donors do not survive contact with a workflow the designers do not share.</p>

<h3 class="case-h3">Smaller decisions, all traceable to fieldwork</h3>
<ul class="design-list">
  <li>The interface was translated into Urdu, with the Noori Nastaliq font after field observation that vaccinators were tilting their phones to read a less-legible default.</li>
  <li>Photo capture required on-device face detection so vaccinators could not skip the child-photo step by photographing an object.</li>
  <li>Vaccine selection changed from all-or-nothing to per-vaccine checkboxes after vaccinators explained that they sometimes waited to open a multi-dose vial to avoid waste.</li>
  <li>Password reset ran through the district health officer's email rather than SMS or a phone call, because the field team found those channels harder to verify.</li>
</ul>

<p>The app was then evaluated with 50 vaccinators from Sahiwal and Sheikhupura (25 in each district) in a task-based usability study with training sessions in Urdu and Punjabi. Eighty-four percent of vaccinators worked through the child-registration task with full concentration; errors clustered on data-entry fields such as the booklet number and phone number rather than on the app's navigation. This is usability evidence &mdash; it says the app was learnable and usable by the people who would use it. It does not, on its own, say anything about coverage or child health outcomes.</p>
</div>

<h2 class="case-h2">4. What happened in practice</h2>

<div class="case-body">
<p>Three different levels of evidence, deliberately kept separate:</p>

<div class="evidence-grid">
  <div class="evidence-card tier-pilot">
    <div class="evidence-tier">Pilot use</div>
    <h4>Seven-month pilot across two districts</h4>
    <p>According to Naseem's 2021 research statement, Har Zindagi ran a seven-month field pilot in Sahiwal and Sheikhupura with 50 state-appointed vaccinators. Over that pilot, the team's Android app created digital records for more than 20,000 children across more than 90,000 visits.</p>
    <p class="attrib">Source: Naseem, 2021 research statement (child health section). A separate 2017 Punjab government snapshot reports an earlier figure of 16,383 children; the two numbers describe different reporting dates and should not be treated as interchangeable.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="evidence-card tier-adoption">
    <div class="evidence-tier">Design adoption</div>
    <h4>Punjab adopted a modified version of the card</h4>
    <p>Per Naseem's 2021 research statement, the Government of Punjab adopted a modified version of the team's immunization card design; the research statement further reports that approximately four million cards were printed annually. Punjab's own District Delivery Challenge Fund materials document a decision to use the redesigned card to upgrade the existing E-Vacc system in the pilot districts.</p>
    <p class="attrib">Sources: Naseem, 2021 research statement; DDCF 2017 brochure and position paper. Note: the exact scope and dates of province-wide adoption, and independent confirmation of the four-million-cards-per-year figure, are still being verified. See "open questions" below.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="evidence-card tier-research">
    <div class="evidence-tier">Research contribution</div>
    <h4>Two peer-reviewed design papers</h4>
    <p>Har Zindagi produced two ACM papers at ICTD '16 and DEV '16 documenting how a connected paper-and-digital record can be co-designed with caregivers and vaccinators. The papers are also honest about what did not translate &mdash; pictorial guidance on the card did not, on its own, reach lower-literate mothers &mdash; and about designing feedback structures for public-sector workers who fear that critical feedback will be treated as evidence they cannot do their jobs.</p>
    <p class="attrib">
      <a href="{{ base_path }}/files/2016_Immunization card redesign.pdf">Batool, Ali, Razaq &amp; Naseem, ICTD '16</a> &middot; <a href="{{ base_path }}/files/2016_immunization information system.pdf">Razaq, Batool, Ali, Khalid, Saif &amp; Naseem, ACM DEV '16</a>
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="limits-note">
<strong>What this evidence does not show.</strong> Punjab's earlier E-Vacc program, later Sindh-side registries such as Zindagi Mehfooz, and other public-health initiatives all operated alongside Har Zindagi. Regional trends in immunization coverage or polio incidence cannot be attributed to Har Zindagi on the basis of this work. A number of cards printed is not the same as a number of children vaccinated or lives saved, and this page does not treat it as such.
</div>
