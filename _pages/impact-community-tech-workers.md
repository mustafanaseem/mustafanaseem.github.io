<p class="project-intro">Community Tech Workers (CTWs) are trusted neighbors trained and paid to help families use online services. Drawing on the community health worker model, they help people apply for rental assistance, book medical appointments, and answer letters in English.</p>

<p class="project-intro">A $1.42 million NSF grant supported our team at the University of Michigan in developing and testing the model with a Detroit public housing community and Afghan families resettled in Washtenaw County. I led the Afghan site, initially in partnership with Jewish Family Services of Washtenaw County and later independently.</p>

<h3 class="project-h3">Working with Afghan families</h3>
<p>Interviews with 14 recently arrived Afghan refugees, conducted by Amna Batool, informed the program. Families relied on friends and relatives for help with online services, often seeking someone who shared their language and understood their household. Women told us they would not respond to an online job posting unless someone they knew vouched for it.</p>

<p>We recruited trusted community members and adapted digital literacy lessons to trainees' experience and languages. The first Afghan cohort trained for 12 weeks. CTWs later held "tech halaqas," small learning circles for women in their homes. A program manager helped handle difficult cases.</p>

<h3 class="project-h3">What we learned</h3>
<p>CTWs reported helping families obtain rental subsidies worth $400&ndash;$600 a month for a year, apply for jobs, and enroll in community college. They also helped people learn to complete tasks themselves. One CTW called a clinic alongside a pregnant woman, explaining each step so she could book her next appointment independently.</p>

<p>A preliminary cost-benefit analysis estimated $299 in benefits per CTW-hour against $58 in costs at the Afghan site, largely from rental subsidies. Detroit's estimates were $42 in benefits against $108 in costs. These figures draw on CTWs' reports, with only three interviews at the Afghan site, and should be treated as early estimates.</p>

<p>The work also exposed gaps in how CTWs were supported. One recalled working about 25 hours in some weeks while being paid for 10. Others took late-night calls about urgent problems. Programs need explicit limits on responsibilities, support for declining requests, and referral options for cases beyond a CTW's role.</p>

<h3 class="project-h3">AI training and scam prevention</h3>
<p>In 2025, we tested a four-week ChatGPT course with eight Afghan newcomers at a public library. Participants reported writing emails, completing forms, and paying bills with less help. Their classroom drafts still contained errors they could not always identify or correct. The study points to a need for training in checking AI responses alongside using the tools.</p>

<p>With a 2025 Google Academic Research Award, I am now leading work on scam prevention through the CTW program. We plan to interview scam survivors, co-design materials in Dari and Pashto, and develop a four-week curriculum.</p>

<h3 class="project-h3">Team and research</h3>
<p>Tawanna Dillahunt led the NSF project, with Julie Hui, Kentaro Toyama, and me as co-principal investigators. Dillahunt and Hui developed the CTW model through earlier work with Detroit small businesses. Amna Batool led the Afghan interview study; Soyoung Lee led the paper on Detroit training.</p>

<p>Murtaza Ahmadi and Emilie Ziebarth were instrumental in delivering the training and running the CTW program with Afghan families. Murtaza was a Bachelor of Science in Information (BSI) student, and Emilie was a Master of Public Health (MPH) student. Both have since graduated.</p>

<p>Published work covers Afghan families' adaptation after resettlement, Detroit's training program, and a measure of community digital capacity. Manuscripts on costs and benefits, CTW labor, and AI training are under review or in preparation.</p>
