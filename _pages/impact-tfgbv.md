<p class="project-intro">Technology-facilitated gender-based violence (TFGBV) can affect students' education, relationships, and safety. Harassment in a class WhatsApp group or the misuse of a photograph can lead to consequences at home and on campus. Seeking help may carry further risks if students expect blame, retaliation, or restrictions on their education.</p>

<p class="project-intro">I serve as principal investigator for this collaboration with Aga Khan University, with Dr. Naghma Rizvi as principal investigator in Pakistan. Together, we are studying these experiences and developing prevention and support for university students. An initial seed grant supported survey and interview research at one public and one private university in Karachi. An Impact Accelerator grant now supports the development and piloting of an intervention with students and organizations that can help them.</p>

<h3 class="project-h3">What we have learned</h3>
<p>Our survey study collected 854 responses. We adapted existing measures through student interviews, expert review, and translation into Urdu to capture forms of abuse that standard questionnaires can miss. The instrument includes coercion by family members and peers, as well as the misuse of photographs that are not sexually explicit. Participants could skip sensitive questions and received information about support resources.</p>

<p>Interviews with 22 students examined how abuse affected their lives and their decisions about seeking help. Students described academic disruption, distress, and withdrawal from social activities. Some feared that telling their families would lead to restrictions on their mobility or education. Others doubted that university reporting systems would protect their privacy or respond effectively. Trusted friends and relatives often provided practical help when formal support felt inaccessible.</p>

<p>These findings inform our approach to prevention. Digital safety education needs to account for the social consequences of reporting abuse and the responsibilities of the institutions students turn to for help. The survey and interview findings are reported in research manuscripts.</p>

<h3 class="project-h3">Developing the intervention</h3>
<p>The Impact Accelerator project brings students together with university staff, NGO partners, and technical experts to design prevention and support suited to their circumstances. The planned pilot will involve 200 students at Aga Khan University and assess whether the intervention is feasible and acceptable, alongside changes in knowledge, attitudes, and digital safety practices. This stage will guide revisions and a future evaluation of effectiveness.</p>

<h3 class="project-h3">Team</h3>
<p>The project brings together researchers in information, public health, and nursing. Alongside Dr. Naghma Rizvi and me, the team includes K. Rivet Amico, Elizabeth King, Michelle Munro-Kramer, Angubeen Khan, Tazeen Ali, and Zahra Tharani.</p>
